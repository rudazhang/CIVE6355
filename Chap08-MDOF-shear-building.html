1 · A column of [K]: hold all floors, push one Paz 7.1; Eq. 7.5

Floor j (tap one to choose it) moved one unit, the others clamped; arrows: the forces that hold the shape. Only the two stories next to floor j deform, so only floors j − 1, j, j + 1 need a force: [K] is tridiagonal. The column sums to zero, except j = 1, where the ground takes −k₁. The dotted row matches the boxed column: kij = kji.

2 · A column of [R]: push one floor, let all move not in Paz; 2024 notes L20

A unit static force at floor j, every floor free; numbers in the stories: the shear. Stories below floor j carry the whole unit shear, stories above carry none, so the floors above ride along. Every floor moves, so [R] is dense: a sparse [K] has a dense inverse. Outlines: cells of equal value, the L-shapes.

6 · Rigid floors against bending beams Paz 7.1 assumptions; Chopra 9.3, Fig. 1.3.3

Same columns, same load, one scale; ticks show joint rotation. Rigid beams keep every joint square, so each column bends in double curvature with k = 12EI/h³. As ρ falls the joints rotate and the frame softens, up to ×4 for one story (6EIc/h³ against 24EIc/h³). With axial strain a tall frame also bends as a whole. Paz's third assumption reads two ways: no P-Δ (tab B) and no axial strain (the toggle).

7 · What it does to [K] and [R] Chopra Eq. 9.3.5

Once the joints rotate, a floor's displacement pulls on floors two and three away, so the frame's [K] fills in; tridiagonal is a property of the model, not of buildings. [R] is dense either way. The shear building's drift is largest at the bottom, where the story shear is; with axial strain it spreads up the height, toward a cantilever's profile.
